Motivation

Motivation: Why simulate patients?

Robotic assistive devices could improve mobility after neurological injury, but tuning their controllers takes long, costly human experiments.

  • The people who would benefit most are often least able to walk for hours
  • Muscle-driven imitation learning (IL): train a policy to reproduce recorded walking on a musculoskeletal model, then use the simulation in place of the participant

The simulation is only as good as the muscle-level solution the policy finds.

[~35 s]

Robotic assistive devices could make a real difference in mobility after a stroke or spinal cord injury. The bottleneck is tuning their controllers, which takes long, expensive human experiments, and the people who would benefit most are usually the least able to walk for hours.

[click] One alternative is muscle-driven imitation learning. We train a policy to reproduce a person’s recorded walking on a musculoskeletal model, and then use that simulation in place of the participant.

[click] But the simulation is only useful if the muscle activity underneath the motion is right, not just the motion itself.

[~40 s]

We saw this directly in our healthy-gait benchmark. We trained two state-of-the-art frameworks, HyFyDy and MuJoCo, to track the same healthy walking, and compared their simulated muscle activations to measured EMG.

HyFyDy had a pooled RMSE of 0.164 and a correlation of 0.40. MuJoCo had roughly double the error, 0.344, and almost no correlation, 0.11.

[click] Both tracked the joint angles, but they got there with very different muscle activity. So kinematic agreement on its own tells us little about whether the muscles are doing the right thing.

Motivation:The problem with pathological gait

Healthy gait

The imitation and effort objectives agree, since healthy walking is close to metabolically optimal.

After stroke or incomplete SCI

The kinematics come from inefficient neural drive, so the two objectives conflict.

An effort-minimizing policy tracking patient kinematics may reproduce the joint angles through a mechanism the patient does not use.

[~35 s]

In healthy walking that gap is manageable. The policy’s reward combines an imitation term and an effort penalty, and healthy gait is close to metabolically optimal, so the two objectives point the same way.

[click] After a stroke or an incomplete spinal cord injury, they don’t. The patient’s kinematics come from inefficient neural drive.

[click] So a policy that minimizes effort while tracking patient kinematics may find an efficient way to reproduce the same joint angles, using a muscle strategy the patient doesn’t actually use. That’s the core risk this project tests.

Objectives

Objectives and Hypotheses

Determine whether the wrong muscle solution occurs, how to detect it, and what prevents it.

H1 Baseline policies track stroke kinematics but match EMG less closely than matched able-bodied controls, especially on the paretic side.

H2 Synergy constraints improve EMG fidelity over the baseline without a meaningful loss in kinematic tracking.

H3 Scaling the model to body dimensions improves tracking, with a smaller effect on EMG fidelity than the controller change.

[~35 s]

The goal is to find out whether that wrong muscle solution happens, how to detect it, and what prevents it. We have three hypotheses.

[click] First, baseline policies will track stroke kinematics but match EMG less closely than in matched able-bodied controls, and the gap will be largest on the paretic side.

[click] Second, constraining the controller with muscle synergies will improve EMG fidelity without meaningfully hurting kinematic tracking.

[click] Third, scaling the model to each person’s body will improve tracking, but will matter less for EMG fidelity than the controller change.

[~45 s]

To test these, we use a two-by-two within-participant design. The rows are the controller and the columns are the model.

The baseline controller outputs one command per muscle, which is our existing pipeline unchanged from the healthy benchmark. Comparing it with matched controls tests H1.

The synergy controller outputs only a few commands, mapped to muscles through weights taken from the participant’s own EMG. Going down a column is H2.

Going across a row, from the generic HyFyDy model to one scaled with OpenSim scale factors, is H3. The bottom-right cell lets us compare the two effect sizes.

Every participant runs all four conditions. One caveat: personalization is geometric only. We aren’t modeling weakness, spasticity, or muscle properties.

Synergy controller

Non-negative matrix factorization per leg over six leg muscles, using the fewest synergies that explain at least 90% of EMG variance. The policy still learns when each group activates.

Primary outcome

Data: [2]. Synergy rationale: [3], [4].

[~45 s]

We use the public Van Criekinge dataset: five stroke survivors, each paired with a matched control. First, we split strides into training and held-out sets, so the synergy weights never see the strides we evaluate on. Marker data becomes the reference motion, and training uses the benchmark’s reward.

For the synergy controller, we run non-negative matrix factorization per leg over six leg muscles, keeping the fewest synergies that explain 90% of the EMG variance. Timing is not prescribed; the policy still learns when each group fires.

The primary outcome is EMG fidelity across the gait cycle, per muscle and per leg, with paretic and non-paretic legs reported separately. Kinematic tracking is a check we have to pass, not evidence of a correct muscle solution.

[~45 s]

Here’s where things stand. We adopted the Van Criekinge dataset, with 138 able-bodied adults and 50 stroke survivors, as our primary data source, and trained the model to walk on one stroke survivor’s reference motion. To be clear, that shows feasibility only; we haven’t yet evaluated it against their kinematics or EMG. We’ve also computed synergies and automated model scaling.

Next, we score that first policy, which also tests the evaluation pipeline. Then we select and convert participants, train all four conditions, and finally validate the most informative ones on Shepherd Center data.

[close, ~10 s] In short, we’re asking whether a simulated patient that walks right is also using the right muscles, and what it takes to make it so. Thank you, I’m happy to take questions.
